John Fitzgerald Kennedy (May 29, 1917, Brookline, Massachusetts, USA – November 22, 1963, Dallas, Texas, USA), also known as JFK, was an American politician and statesman, the 35th President of the United States (1961–63) from the Democratic Party. He was a member of the U.S. House of Representatives from Massachusetts (1947–53) and a Senator from Massachusetts from 1953–60. His presidency fell during a particularly tense period of the Cold War. According to most citizens of the country, Kennedy is one of the ten greatest American presidents in history. He was born into an intelligent, wealthy family in Massachusetts. He graduated from Harvard University (B.A. in political science, 1939). After the United States entered World War II, he volunteered for military service and later became a torpedo boat commander in the Pacific War against Japan, and in 1945, he became a lieutenant in the U.S. Navy.
 After the war, he entered the House of Representatives from Massachusetts (1947–1953), known for his decisive vote in acquitting physicist Robert Oppenheimer of charges of working for the USSR, and from 1953 to 1960, he represented his state in the U.S. Senate. In November 1960, in the next presidential election, the 43-year-old Democrat Kennedy narrowly defeated Republican Richard Nixon, the vice president of the United States, replacing another Republican elected in 1953, D. Eisenhower, who turned 70 in 1960. He became the first Catholic president in US history.
 Kennedy's nearly three-year presidency was marked by the Berlin Crisis, the Cuban Missile Crisis, the Bay of Pigs operation, the space race between the USSR and the USA, which led to the start of the Apollo space program, as well as serious steps to overcome racial discrimination. Time's Person of the Year (1961).
 In the modern public consciousness, Kennedy is most often associated with his assassination, which shocked the entire world, and numerous hypotheses for its resolution are put forward to this day.
 
 
 == Biography ==
 
 
 === Family ===
 
 Maternal grandfather - John Francis Fitzgerald (1863-1950), eloquent politician, three-time mayor of Boston. He graduated from Boston College, and in 1894 he was elected to the US Congress. From 1906 to 1914, he served as mayor of Boston, regularly yielding the position to other politicians as his terms expired. He remained one of the most prominent political figures in Massachusetts until the end of his life, predicting that his grandson John would become president. He married his second cousin, Mary Josephine Hannon, and had six children. His paternal grandfather, Patrick Joseph Kennedy (1858–1929), was an entrepreneur and politician who was elected to the U.S. House of Representatives from Massachusetts. At fourteen, he dropped out of school and went to work because the family had no means of support. Over time, he used his earnings to open a small chain of bars and eateries, and founded a liquor and coal company. He married the daughter of a bar owner, Mary Hickey, and had four children. His mother, Rose Elizabeth Fitzgerald (1890–1995), was a philanthropist and matriarch of the Kennedy clan. She attended a Catholic school and college in Manhattanville.
 Father - Joseph Patrick Kennedy (1888-1969), financier, businessman and politician, patriarch of the Kennedy clan, US Ambassador to Great Britain.
 He studied at the Boston Latin School and graduated from Harvard University. At a young age, he became president of the board of Columbia Trust Bank, doubling its capital.
 Joseph and Rose met in 1906, but the girl, according to her father's plan, was to marry another young man, whom she categorically disliked.
 In October 1914, Joseph and Rose married and moved permanently to the city of Brookline, where a year later their first child, Joseph Patrick Kennedy Jr., was born.
 
 Kennedy Sr. believed that banking was above all else and, as he later wrote in his memoirs, "all roads are open to a banker, since he plays an important role in the development of any entrepreneurial activity."
  Joseph didn't plan to be a major figure in his hometown; he wanted to reach a higher level—the banking industry in Boston and New York. His plans were thwarted by World War I. He left the bank and joined the steel and shipbuilding company Bethlehem Steel in Quincy, thus avoiding conscription. One of his colleagues later said: "In the mid-1920s, Kennedy became a member of the brokerage firm Bramin, thereby becoming one of the most successful investors of his generation." Joseph's relentless climb up the corporate ladder repelled Rose; she longed for a more orderly and peaceful family life. By the early 1930s, she had already given birth to nine children and was worried about her large family after doctors discovered that her eldest daughter, Rosemary, was lagging behind her peers in mental development. To find some relief from her family problems, Rose traveled extensively throughout the United States and Europe. Joseph often cheated on his wife, in particular with the silent film star, three-time Oscar nominee Gloria Swanson, in films starring which he often invested his own money.
 At the peak of his career, Kennedy Sr. was friends with Pope Pius XII, newspaper magnate W.R. Hearst, and was a personal adviser to US President Franklin Delano Roosevelt. Joseph expected that his eldest son, Joe Jr., would follow his own path in life and placed all his hopes on him, not John.
 As historian and long-time Columbia University professor Alan Brinkley notes, "long before members of the Kennedy clan became well-known political figures, the family had already entered the ranks of the most famous Irish families in America."
 
 
 === Birth and Early Years ===
 John Fitzgerald Kennedy, the second of Joseph and Rose's children, was born in Brookline, on Beals Street, at 3 a.m. on May 29, 1917, into a wealthy family of Irish descent. The boy was named after John the Apostle and Rose's father, John Francis Fitzgerald. Following an old American tradition, John's family called him Jack. Soon after John's birth, the family moved from their cramped home to a large mansion on Abbotsford Road. There, he attended Dexter School, where he and his older brother, Joseph, were the only Catholics. As a child, John was frail, suffering from a variety of illnesses, from chickenpox to scarlet fever, which nearly killed him. Kennedy's most vivid childhood memory is touring the polls with his grandfather John in 1922, when he was running for governor.
 Having become a major economic figure in the American heartland and with a net worth of $2 million, in 1927 Joseph Kennedy moved his family to the stock market capital of New York City (or rather, to its mini-neighborhood of Riverdale), and then to Bronxville. In Massachusetts, the elder Kennedy retained property—the family estate in the small village of Hyannis Port. There, John began attending Riverdale Country School, where he was neither a good nor a bad student.
 
 
 === Secondary Education ===
 In the fall of 1930, thirteen-year-old John was sent to Canterbury Catholic School, which was located far from home—in New Milford, Connecticut. He continued to be regularly ill and missed his family, complaining in letters that he was being "harassed with religion" at school; the only time he could go off the grounds was when Yale played Harvard or the Army. John spent almost the entire school year in the hospital, and in his final months, he was homeschooled. Despite his illnesses, he was active in sports at school, participating in baseball, basketball, and track. Kennedy began ninth grade at Choate Rosemary Hall, a private boarding school where his brother Joseph had already attended, and before that, his future political colleagues Adlai Stevenson II and Chester Bowles. John also failed to earn high grades at Choate; according to historian Alan Brinkley, "his work was sloppy, and he had a reputation for being frivolous and unorganized at a school that elevated order to a principle." Kennedy often referred to Choate as a prison; his health did not improve, and he spent long periods of time in the renowned Mayo Clinic. A rebel by nature, Kennedy joined the so-called "Mackers Club," where members performed obscene songs about teachers and administration. Despite his defiant behavior, Kennedy was not expelled from school, and he graduated, albeit not with a perfect diploma. Higher Education In 1935, he entered Harvard University, but at the very end of August, he decided to attend the London School of Economics and Political Science instead, to personally study with the eminent economist Professor Harold Laski, who later spoke warmly of Kennedy. In the English capital, John fell ill again, this time with jaundice, and returned home, where he enrolled at Princeton University, in part because his best friend, Lem Billings, was already studying there. Kennedy found Princeton "an oppressively provincial little university town." After failing his first semester, he was again admitted to a Boston hospital with an illness unknown to doctors. For several weeks, John underwent tests and examinations, which he later called "the most difficult ordeal of my entire storm-battered life." Ultimately, the young man was diagnosed with leukemia. Kennedy was incredulous, but he was proven right—the doctors soon admitted they had made a mistake. John spent the rest of the school year at a resort in Palm Beach, on a ranch in Arizona, and in Los Angeles. In August 1936, he was readmitted to Harvard University, whose admissions committee concluded: "Jack possesses excellent intellectual abilities but lacks a deep interest in learning... There is reason to believe he may be accepted."
 
 John excelled at Harvard than at Choate or Princeton, read extensively, and continued to play sports. Kennedy spent the summer of 1937 on a major European tour with Lem Billings, sponsored by his father. He also arranged John's introduction to the future Pope, Cardinal Pacelli, and several other major world figures. The young man was particularly impressed by countries with totalitarian regimes, particularly Italy and Germany. Upon returning from the cruise, a smitten Kennedy became seriously interested in history and political science. He was eager to excel not only in his studies but also in student society, setting himself the goal of joining one of Harvard's social clubs. He soon became a member of the Hasty Pudding Club and contributed to the university newspaper, The Harvard Crimson. However, John was most proud of his membership in the Spee Club, spending almost all of his free time at its headquarters. Kennedy learned of the outbreak of World War II while vacationing at a resort in Antibes. Returning to Harvard, he titled his senior thesis "Appeasement in Munich," and was assisted in writing by a whole team: from his father's assistants to stenographers and typists. "A poorly written, but conscientious, engaging, and intelligent analysis of a complex problem"—that was the verdict of Kennedy's academic advisors. Despite the mediocrity of this thesis, it was published as a book, with the help of New York Times journalist Arthur Krock, under the different title "Why England Slept." The young Kennedy's analytical work provoked a widespread public response, which, according to Alan Brinkley, was driven by "the almost complete lack of interest among political analysts of the time in the question of the readiness of democratic states to resist totalitarian regimes." In it, John also first mentioned the thesis that would later become one of the key points of his political doctrine: "Democracy must be strong and combative to withstand the hardships of a long, arduous struggle with the growing communist world."
 The book was published in the United States and England with a total circulation of 80,000 copies, and the author's fee was 40,000 dollars.
 
 
 ===World War II ===
 After receiving his bachelor's degree from Harvard University, Kennedy pondered what to do next. He considered studying law; in 1941, he applied to Yale University and even studied for a few months at Stanford University, but soon America officially entered World War II. John knew that he would not be accepted into the army due to his ill health. A year before the events at Pearl Harbor, he attempted to pass the medical examination and was found unfit due to a back injury. With the help of his father and his father's acquaintances (particularly Admiral Alan Kirk), Kennedy was sent to the Office of Naval Intelligence in Washington in October. Kennedy prepared reports for headquarters and found the work boring. He craved real military action, according to historian Alan Brinkley: After a brief stint at the Intelligence Agency, John was transferred to the Charleston, South Carolina, Navy Yard. In July 1942, he joined the Naval Officer Training School (Chicago, Illinois). In Portsmouth, Rhode Island, he learned the basics of naval torpedo boat operations and assumed command of PT-109 in the spring of 1943. Prior to this, dreaming of becoming its commander, Kennedy again turned to his father and Massachusetts Senator David E. Walsh for help. John was immediately transferred to the Pacific Ocean, where hostilities between the United States and Japan were in full swing. As a result, he became a World War II veteran with the rank of lieutenant. Kennedy served throughout the Solomon Islands campaign as commander of the torpedo boat PT-109. He received numerous awards for his bravery during the war. On August 2, Kennedy was assigned to a group of sixteen boats to attack Japanese ships. During a night raid, an enemy destroyer emerged from the darkness and rammed PT-109, cutting it in half. John's already injured back was severely injured when he fell to the deck. Two of the thirteen sailors were killed instantly; the rest were saved thanks to Kennedy's timely and decisive actions. The cutter's crew swam for five hours to the nearest shore, with Kennedy dragging one of the wounded men along. On Nauro Island, John carved a small message on a coconut shell giving the cutter's crew's coordinates. A week later, Kennedy and his men departed for home on another torpedo boat from the New Zealand patrol in New Georgia. In the following days, the American press wrote admiringly about Kennedy and his crew's heroism, often referring to John as "Kennedy's son." For his courage during combat, John was awarded numerous orders and medals, including the Purple Heart and the Navy and Marine Corps Medal. Admiral William Halsey personally signed the order honoring Kennedy: "His courage, endurance, and leadership qualities helped save several lives in keeping with the highest traditions of the United States naval service." Ten days after the PT-109 incident, Kennedy returned to the front. In December 1943, he contracted malaria, his back injury recurred, and due to his critical health, John decided to return home. In the new year of 1944, Kennedy arrived in San Francisco and was hospitalized at the Mayo Clinic, where he remained for several months. In March 1945, a few months before the end of the war, he was officially discharged.
 
 
 === The Start of a Political Career ===
 
 A few months after his discharge, Kennedy took up journalism, covering the creation of the United Nations in San Francisco for W.R. Hearst's media conglomerate, the Hearst Corporation. He then embarked on another tour of Europe, during which he reconsidered the key political events and personalities of the time.
 After the death of his eldest child, Joseph, in August 1944, all hopes in the family were pinned on John. Upon his return from Europe, his father began to persuade him to enter politics, although he doubted his political inclinations. John knew for sure that he would not go into journalism. Kennedy Sr. helped lay the foundation for his son's future political career by contacting Massachusetts Congressman James Michael Curley in the U.S. House of Representatives, offering to vacate his House seat in exchange for resolving some of his problems. Thus, John F. Kennedy entered the U.S. House of Representatives and began his political career. From 1947 to 1953, Kennedy represented the Boston District in the U.S. Congress as a Democratic congressman. Congressman Kennedy served as a member of the House Education and Labor Relations Committee. In 1953, Kennedy became a senator, defeating Senator Henry Lodge in a hard-fought race. Senator Kennedy was a member of the Labor and Public Welfare Committee. The future president's most controversial decision during this period was his decision to abstain from the Senate vote to censure Senator Joseph McCarthy for his improprieties during open hearings on espionage in the armed forces. Scholars offer various motivations for this move (including a hospital stay and a desire not to undermine the trust of conservative voters), but Kennedy's own statement from 1960 is also well known: I never called myself perfect. I made the usual quota of mistakes for a politician. The Joe McCarthy Case? I was in a no-win situation. My brother worked for Joe. I was against it, I didn't want him to work for Joe, but he did. And how the hell could I stand up and denounce Joe McCarthy when my own brother worked for him? So it wasn't so much a matter of political duty as it was a personal problem.
 
 
 === The 1960 Presidential Campaign ===
 
 When John F. Kennedy, the Democratic presidential nominee, won the 1960 election, he was 43 years old. During the campaign, Kennedy put forward the "New Frontier" concept (similar to Roosevelt's "New Deal"), which later largely served as the platform for his presidency. On January 2, 1960, Kennedy officially announced his candidacy. In the Democratic primaries, he faced Senator Hubert Humphrey of Minnesota, Senator Stuart Symington of Missouri, Senate Majority Leader Lyndon Johnson of Texas, and Adlai Stevenson. By the time the convention opened in Los Angeles (July 11–15), Kennedy had already secured victory and was confirmed on the first ballot. Two weeks later, the Republicans chose Vice President Richard Nixon as their nominee. In the first-ever televised debate with his opponent, Richard Nixon, Kennedy came across as a businesslike, eloquent, and energetic man. During the campaign, he spoke of the need to resolutely move forward into the new decade, for "new frontiers are within reach—whether we seek them or not." Kennedy concentrated his efforts on the populous states of the Northeast, counting on his running mate, Senator Johnson, to secure the Democrats' traditional Southern support. This strategy was successful, but the margin of victory was insignificant. Kennedy defeated Nixon by a majority of 119,000 popular votes (out of 69 million voters). Kennedy and Johnson received 303 electoral votes, Nixon and Lodge received 219, and Senator Harry Flood Byrd received 15. According to the press, it was not his party's political platform or expectations of "energetic leadership" and Kennedy's promised policy of "flexible response" to the challenges of the outside world that played a decisive role in Kennedy's victory, but the way he looked on television.
 Kennedy was to become the first Catholic president in the country's history (the second was Joe Biden, elected 60 years later).
 
 
 === President of the United States (1961-1963) ===
 
 John Kennedy was elected president on November 8, 1960.
 
 "The Kennedy administration will be able to take a number of steps "in the right direction" (regarding the possibility of improving American-Soviet relations), but this will be done only gradually. "It is difficult to expect fundamental changes in US policy in the near future, since Kennedy will be bound by certain obligations regarding the continuity of foreign policy" (Cyrus Eaton, 1960).
 On January 20, 1961, John Kennedy took the oath of office and thus became the 35th President of the United States. Kennedy concluded his first inaugural address with the appeal: "Think not of what your country can give you, but of what you can give it." The new president's government included completely new people with connections in the financial and monopoly circles of the United States, or people who had already succeeded in the political arena.
 The Kennedy administration included: Vice President Lyndon Johnson, Secretary of State D. Rusk (a specialist in political science, served in the Pentagon, the State Department, and since 1952 headed the Rockefeller Foundation), Secretary of Defense R. McNamara (a professional businessman, president of the Ford concern), Secretary of the Treasury D. Dillon (served in the Eisenhower administration), and Attorney General Robert Kennedy (Kennedy's brother, managed the election campaign).
 Of the first 200 people appointed by Kennedy to the highest government posts, about half came from the government apparatus, 18% were university professors, 6% were businessmen, which contrasted sharply with the composition of the administration of his predecessor, Eisenhower, where only 6% were university professors, and 42% by businessmen.
 The White House regularly hosted chamber concerts by outstanding musicians.
 President Kennedy donated his entire government salary to charitable causes.
 
 
 ==== Domestic Policy ====
 According to the World Bank, US GDP increased from $543 billion to $685 billion from 1960 to 1964, with an average annual GDP growth rate of 6% and an average annual inflation rate of 1%.
 The beginning of Kennedy's presidency coincided with a phase of cyclical economic growth. However, by the spring of 1962, the economic situation had noticeably worsened: growth slowed, unemployment, which had begun to decline, stalled at 5.5%, and the volume of new capital investment also declined. In May, this was compounded by a drop in stock prices—the sharpest since 1929. One of the new administration's top priorities was ending the economic downturn, but Kennedy lost the trust of business by pushing for lower steel prices in 1962, which the government considered excessive. The administration clashed with steel companies, led by the United States Steel Corporation, which, despite the administration's insistence that the steelworkers' union limit its wage demands to "guidelines," decided to drastically increase steel prices. Only by using all its leverage was the White House able to reverse this decision, at the cost of worsening relations with the monopolies. He achieved this immediate goal, but lost the strong support of the industrialists. For example, in January 1963, Kennedy submitted to Congress a program to cut corporate taxes (from 52 to 47%) and personal income tax rates (from 20-91 to 14-65%), totaling approximately $10 billion, while effectively abandoning tax reform. When Kennedy attempted to push through Congress a tax cut to stimulate savings and revive the economy, conservative opposition dashed any hope of passing a bill that would create a budget deficit. At the same time, he promised to reduce government spending on social needs and balance the federal budget.
 Despite some successes, Kennedy's presidency cannot be called a success in terms of legislation. He failed to secure new appropriations for the development of education and medical care for the elderly, and the minimum wage was raised only slightly. For example, the extension of unemployment benefits in 1961-1962 left more than 3 million unemployed; The increase in the minimum hourly wage (to $1.15 in 1961 and $1.25 in 1963) affected only 3.6 million of the 26.6 million low-wage workers. 
 The government's measures to combat unemployment and create conditions for vocational training and improving the quality of skilled labor training—the Depression Relief Act of 1961, the Displaced Workers Retraining Act of 1962, the Vocational Education Act of 1963, the Economic Opportunity Act of 1964, appropriations for public works, etc.—did not lead to significant improvements in employment. The movement for a shorter (35-hour) workweek gained momentum.
 In 1964, a nationwide food assistance program for the needy was launched; 367,000 people received food stamps from the state.
 Kennedy advocated for equal rights for blacks, taking the model of Abraham Lincoln, supported Martin Luther King, and met with him in Washington in 1963. On June 19, 1963, President Kennedy introduced a civil rights bill to Congress that would prohibit segregation in all public places. One of President Kennedy's decisions was to stop issuing silver coins and silver certificates due to the steadily rising price of silver. In 1963, at his initiative, Congress passed Public Law 88-36, authorizing the Federal Reserve to issue $1 and $2 bills and prohibiting the Treasury from issuing silver certificates. Since the Treasury was still required to issue these certificates during the transition period, Kennedy signed Executive Order 11110 that same day, delegating the president's authority to issue silver certificates to the Treasury. A conspiracy theory erroneously links this order to the issuance of the 1963 U.S. Treasury notes. Thus, it is assumed that Kennedy was going to deprive the Federal Reserve of its monopoly on money emission and therefore this decision allegedly became the cause of a conspiracy against the president.
 By order of President Kennedy, the National Endowment for the Arts of the United States was created
 
 
 ==== Foreign Policy ====
 
 Kennedy advocated for improving relations between the United States and the USSR, but his presidency was also marked by great foreign policy tensions: the unsuccessful landing at the Bay of Pigs, the Berlin Crisis, the Cuban Missile Crisis (one of the phrases recorded in the diary of the 35th president, "fear of loss breeds suspicion" - this is how Kennedy himself argued this crisis). On June 4, 1961, in Vienna, at Schönbrunn Palace, Kennedy's only meeting with Soviet leader Khrushchev took place.
 Under Kennedy, US intervention in the civil war in South Vietnam intensified; in 1961, he sent the first regular units of the US armed forces to South Vietnam (previously only military advisers had served there). By the end of 1963, the United States had spent $3 billion on the Vietnam War, and 16,000 U.S. soldiers and officers were stationed in South Vietnam. Kennedy, however, opposed excessive intervention in Vietnam, and military action escalated under his successor. He sanctioned an environmental war in Vietnam, which led to catastrophic environmental consequences in the region. In March 1961, the Peace Corps was created to provide volunteer assistance to people in developing countries by eradicating illiteracy and acquiring basic work skills and knowledge. On March 13, 1961, Kennedy announced the Alliance for Progress program, designed to promote the economic and political development of Latin American countries. The official goals of this program were to ensure an annual increase in industrial production in Latin American countries of at least 2.5% per year, eliminate illiteracy on the continent, and implement agrarian reforms. The program was to be funded with $20 billion over a ten-year period, which was almost ten times the total amount of American aid to Latin America from 1945 to 1960. In 1961, Kennedy created the United States Agency for International Development to help address the economic and political problems of developing countries. By order of President Kennedy, the Arms Control and Disarmament Agency was created in 1961. Kennedy contributed greatly to space exploration, initiating the Apollo program ("We choose to go to the moon"). He proposed to First Secretary of the CPSU Central Committee Khrushchev to join forces in preparing for a lunar flight, but he refused. On August 5, 1963, in Moscow, representatives of the USSR, the USA, and Great Britain signed a treaty banning nuclear weapons tests in three areas: in the air, on land, and under water. On October 17, representatives of the USSR and the USA voted unanimously for the UN General Assembly's resolution banning the launch of objects carrying nuclear weapons into orbit.
 In October 1963, President Kennedy approved the sale of $250 million in grain to the Soviet Union, which helped the Soviet leadership cope with the consequences of a serious crop failure. 
 Another achievement of 1963 was that the USSR and the USA began preparations for the "Pan-European Conference on Security and Cooperation in Europe."
 Kennedy's political testament is his speech at American University on June 10, 1963, which called for "ensuring peace not only in our time, but forever" by "expanding mutual understanding between the USSR and ourselves."
 
 
 === Death ===
 
 John Kennedy was assassinated on November 22, 1963, around 12:30 PM in Dallas, Texas. Shots were heard as the presidential motorcade moved through the streets of the city. The first bullet from a sniper rifle hit the president in the back of the neck and exited the front of the throat; the second entered his head, causing a fracture of the skull bones in the back of the head and brain damage. President Kennedy was taken to the operating room, where he died half an hour after the assassination attempt at the age of 47. Texas Governor Connally, who was riding in the same car, was also seriously wounded, and a bystander was also slightly wounded.
 The shots were fired from the window of the Texas School Book Depository by shooter Lee Harvey Oswald, who had been talking to a KGB agent two months before the assassination attempt on Kennedy. He was arrested on suspicion of assassination and was shot dead two days later while leaving the police station under heavy police escort by Dallas resident Jack Ruby, who also later died in prison.
 The official Warren Commission report on the Kennedy assassination was published in 1964; according to this report, Oswald was the president's assassin and all the shots were fired from the top floor of the School Book Depository building. According to the report, no conspiracy to commit assassination could be uncovered. Official data on the Kennedy assassination are contradictory and contain a number of "blank spots." There are a particularly large number of conspiracy theories regarding this case: it is doubted that Oswald fired at the car at all or that he was the only shooter. A connection between the assassination and various major political and business figures is assumed, the deliberate elimination of witnesses is seen, etc. (see, for example, Norman Lewis's novel "The Sicilian Specialist"). Soviet intelligence believed that Kennedy's then "number two," Vice President Lyndon Johnson, was behind the assassination. One of the theories about the circumstances of the assassination is presented in the film "JFK: Shots in Dallas" by Oliver Stone. Films about John F. Kennedy include: "PT 109" (1963) - about Kennedy's participation in World War II; The Kennedys and The Kennedys (1983 and 2011) series; John F. Kennedy: Reckless Youth (1993).  ==== Affair with Marilyn Monroe ====
 The president is also credited with an affair with Hollywood actress Marilyn Monroe. In 1946, Marilyn met then-Senator John Kennedy through their mutual friend Robert Stack. They began an affair that lasted until the summer of 1962. There are numerous accounts of this relationship. Arthur James, who met Marilyn through Charlie Chaplin in the late 1940s, recalled: “Even though Jack was a senator, he was practically unknown here, and he and Marilyn could remain unnoticed. Sometimes they would go for a drink at the Malibu Cottage, the filthiest place on earth. It was just a bar with, I think, eight seats and sawdust on the floor, but at that time it became the hangout of Hollywood’s biggest celebrities.” The actress told James that she and Kennedy would secretly rent a room at the Holiday House Motel in Malibu or another hotel. It was a place for secret rendezvous between the lovers. "I don't think these meetings meant anything to John Kennedy then, but Marilyn never forgot it and worried about it, especially later, when he became president. Later, Peter Lawford's house in Santa Monica became their residence."

 == Memory ==
 A large number of objects, streets, schools, and other things (for example, the international airport in New York) are named after Kennedy in the United States. Kennedy is considered by most citizens of the country to be one of the ten greatest American presidents in history.
 
 Kennedy's portrait is depicted on the 50-cent coin issued in 1964.
 In 1963, New York's Idlewild International Airport was renamed John F. Kennedy International Airport. At the same time, the airport code was replaced with the letter combination JFK (the initials of John Fitzgerald Kennedy).
 In 1966, the Harvard School of Government, one of the faculties of Harvard University, was named after Kennedy.
 The aircraft carrier USS John F. Kennedy (CV-67) is named after him.
 The NASA space center located at Cape Canaveral is named after him.
 An island in the Pacific Ocean and a mountain in North America are named after Kennedy.
